# Overview

## Are these models appropriate for my data?
If one or more of following characteristics apply to your dataset, these models may be an appropriate tool to assist in status and trend analyses:

- Longitudinal data (e.g., annual data collected over many years)
- Hierarchical data (e.g., transects or plots within sites, sites within strata, and strata within parks)
- Complex survey designs (e.g., stratification, rotating panels with data missing by design, unequal probability of including sites in the sampling design)
- The presence of “nuisance” variables that may influence detection, (e.g., botanist experience, timing of spring green-up)
- Any of the following data types: counts (e.g., richness, density), ordinal (e.g., cover classes, soil stability), hits/total (e.g., point intercept), presence/absence (e.g., frequency), censored or truncated (e.g., canopy gaps)
- Covariates that could explain variation in the data (e.g., climate, topography, fire, grazing)
- A desire to forecast expected condition into the future

Depending on the complexity of the design, Bayesian hierarchical modeling is not always necessary, and it does have trade-offs. It requires more statistical expertise and substantially longer computation times. However, it can solve several of the problems inherent in the complex sampling designs for long-term natural resource monitoring in parks.

## How have these models been used?

Several National Park Service Inventory & Monitoring (I&M) networks used these models in trend analyses of vegetation and soils data:

- [Little Bighorn Battlefield National Monument vegetation composition, structure, and soils: 2009–2016 status and trend, revised](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/Reference/Profile/2266871)
- [Grant-Kohrs Ranch National Historic Site vegetation composition, structure, and soils: 2009–2019 status and trend](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/Reference/Profile/2293402)
- [Vegetation and Soil Trends, Black Canyon of the Gunnison National Park and Curecanti National Recreation Area, 2011–2022](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/Reference/Profile/2309325)
- [Status and trend of upland vegetation and soils at Dinosaur National Monument, 2010–2020](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/Reference/Profile/2302751)
- [Status and trends in upland vegetation and soils at Capitol Reef National Park, 2009–2018](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/Reference/Profile/2279505)
- [Petrified Forest grasslands, status and trends: Vegetation and soils monitoring 2007–2018](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/Reference/Profile/2271769)
- [Status and trends in grassland vegetation at Chaco Culture National Historical Park: Upland vegetation and soils monitoring 2007–2018](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/Reference/Profile/2288660)
- [Site characterization and change over time in semi-arid grassland and shrublands at three parks—Chaco Culture National Historic Park, Petrified Forest National Park, and Wupatki National Monument: Upland vegetation and soils monitoring 2007–2021](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/Reference/Profile/2301582)
- [Vegetation trends in pinyon-juniper woodlands in Southern Colorado Plateau Network parks](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/Reference/Profile/2312771)

## Is there a plain language version of the statistics paper?

Yes. The [Mind the Gap: Modeling Missing Data for Complex Survey Designs](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/mind-the-gap-modeling-missing-data-for-complex-survey-designs.htm) web article summarizes the [Zachmann et al. 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13253-021-00473-z) paper.
